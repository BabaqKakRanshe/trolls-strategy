Steam Capsule Art Best Practices: Data From 98 Top Sellers

I tallied 98 Steam top-seller capsules on July 2, 2026: only 20 show a readable face, 44 use dark backgrounds, and 18 fail the 120px legibility test.

August 14, 2026Updated September 15, 202612 min readBy Steam Page Analyzer Team

I downloaded the small capsule for every game on Steam’s global top sellers list on July 2, 2026, and tallied what the art actually does: only 20 of 98 capsules show a face you can read, 44 of 98 sit on a dark background, and 18 fail a basic legibility test at the 120px size Steam renders in list views. That last group is almost entirely famous franchises that can afford to be illegible.

Key facts

  • •20 of 98 top-seller capsules (20%) show a face large enough to read an expression, and 40 of 98 (41%) include any figure, creature or silhouette. Hand tally of Steam’s global top sellers list, July 2, 2026.
  • •44 of 98 (45%) sit on a dark background, measured as mean perceived luminance under 80 on a 0-255 scale; the median capsule sits at 82. Computed from pixels on the same 98 capsules, same date.
  • •18 of 98 (18%) are hard to read or carry no text at all when downscaled to 120x45. Hand assessment of the same 98 capsules, same date.
  • •97 of 98 put the title on the image. That matches Valve’s own instruction that “your logo should nearly fill the small capsule” (Steamworks graphical assets documentation, read September 15, 2026).
  • •The small capsule uploads at 462x174 pixels and Steam renders it as small as 120x45, so legibility at that size is a spec question, not a taste question (same Valve documentation, same date).

Most capsule advice is taste (“make it pop”) or secondhand folklore (“always show a character face”). This post is a tally you can check my work on: the sample, the categories, the counts, and where the counts disagree with the folklore. If you want the step-by-step design process instead, that lives in our capsule design guide; this is the pattern data underneath it.

The sample and the method

The sample is the first 100 items on Steam’s top sellers search, global region, pulled on July 2, 2026. Two of those items were hardware (Steam Deck and the Steam Machine), so the working sample is 98 game listings, which includes a few DLC packs like Cyberpunk 2077: Phantom Liberty because they occupy the same capsule slots and play by the same rules.

For each listing I grabbed the small capsule - the 462x174 asset that the Steamworks graphical assets documentation says gets auto-generated down to 184x69 and 120x45 for denser list views. Search results show it at roughly half size, 231x87. Then two kinds of measurement:

  • •Computed: mean luminance and saturation per capsule, measured from pixels. No judgment involved.
  • •Hand-tallied: does a readable face appear, does any character or creature appear, is the capsule a logotype on a flat background, and is the title readable at 120x45. These are my judgment calls on a single day’s snapshot. Borderline cases exist and I flag them below.

One caveat about the sample: a top-sellers list measures revenue, not capsule quality. Half these games sell on brand recognition, and their capsules get away with choices yours cannot. The useful move is to separate what the whole list does from what the roughly 25 indie and small-studio titles on it do. I do both.

The single no-text capsule was Subnautica 2, running a pure underwater scene with no logo. It is the exception that survives on franchise recognition; the other 97 put the title on the image, exactly as the Steamworks docs require (“logo should nearly fill the small capsule”).

Three counts deserve a closer look, because each one contradicts something I hear from developers weekly.

Faces are much rarer than the advice suggests

“Put a big character face on it” is the most repeated capsule tip in indie circles. On this list it is a minority pattern: 20 of 98 capsules (20%) show a face large enough to read an expression - Crusader Kings III’s king, Leon in Resident Evil 4, Shaq on NBA 2K26, the princess in Slay the Princess, the mascots in R.E.P.O. Another five or so have character faces too small to read (Palworld’s creatures, the fox in TUNIC, Hornet in Silksong).

What is common is character presence: 40 of 98 (41%) include some figure, creature, or silhouette, even when the face is hidden. PUBG uses tiny soldier silhouettes. Counter-Strike 2 and Ready or Not use masked operators. Warframe and Dead Space use helmets. The pattern is “something alive to anchor the scene,” not “eye contact with the shopper.”

Among the ~25 indie titles in the sample, faces do run hotter: Schedule I, PEAK, R.E.P.O., Dave the Diver, Rain World, and Slay the Princess all lead with a readable face or mascot, which is roughly a quarter to a third of the indie subset. That matches the logic Chris Zukowski laid out in his capsule trends research: the job of the art is instant genre recognition, and for character-driven indie games a face is the fastest genre signal available. For a factory sim it is not, and the top sellers reflect that.

Note

The honest version of the face rule is conditional: a face helps when your genre sells on character, tone, or horror. Nobody buys Euro Truck Simulator 2 for a face.

Text is the design, not a caption on the design

Here is the pattern nobody puts on the mood board: for most of these capsules, the title text IS the composition. 24 of 98 are essentially a logotype on a flat or lightly textured background - no scene, no character. Cyberpunk 2077’s yellow slab, Marvel Rivals’ white card, Borderlands 4’s yellow-on-black, Persona 3 Reload’s near-empty white capsule, both Spider-Man games on plain red.

Before you copy that: nearly every capsule in the logotype-only group is a franchise whose name does the selling. A flat capsule that says RUST works because the shopper already knows what Rust is. The same layout with an unknown name is a blank space in the search results. If your game is not famous, the art has to carry the genre signal and the text has to carry the name, together.

The middle path, and the one the successful indies on this list take, is big text plus one clear subject: Dave the Diver (chunky yellow title, pixel diver), PEAK (fat white letters, cartoon climbers), DREDGE (heavy logotype, small boat and lighthouse), Escape the Backrooms (full-width title over a corridor). Text spans more than half the capsule width on most of the 98. Small, tasteful logos are what the famous games do, not what the breakout games do.

The median capsule sits at 82/255 - just on the dark side. Dark is the default costume of AAA action, horror, and ARPGs, and those genres are overrepresented among top sellers. But note the size of the light group: one in five capsules is bright, and because Steam’s own UI is dark blue-grey, a light capsule gets contrast against the storefront for free. Stardew Valley’s sky-blue capsule has been doing this for a decade. About half the sample (49 of 98) also runs heavily saturated color, so “dark” rarely means drab; it usually means a dark base with one loud accent color.

Whichever direction you pick, the title needs contrast against your own background first and Steam’s page second - the exact check our capsule validator runs on your upload.

The 120px squint test: 18 of 98 fail

Steam renders your small capsule at 120x45 in several list contexts. So I downscaled all 98 to exactly that size and sorted them by whether a person who has never heard of the game could read the title at a glance:

Title legibility at 120x45, the smallest size Steam renders
Clearly readable at a glance68 of 98
Readable with effort (stylized or thin type)12 of 98
Hard to read or no text18 of 98
Source: Hand assessment of all 98 top-seller capsules downscaled to 120x45, steampageanalyzer.com, July 2, 2026

About 7 in 10 pass cleanly. The interesting part is who fails: GTA V’s tiny badge, the ornate Kingdom Hearts and Final Fantasy VII logotypes, Call of Duty’s small lockups, Ace Combat 7, Stellar Blade’s script logo, and all four Binding of Isaac items with their scratchy pixel lettering. Every one of those is carried by a name you already know. Among the indie subset, essentially everything except the Isaac franchise items passes - newer indie hits treat small-size legibility as non-negotiable, and the Steamworks docs agree: the logo has to stay readable even at the smallest auto-generated size.

The squint test is the cheapest capsule test that exists. Zoom your capsule to 10% in your art tool, or run it through the capsule validator, which renders your upload at every size Steam uses and lists the exact pixel specs for each capsule slot.

What the leaderboard’s top capsules share

Our public leaderboard ranks the Steam pages we have analyzed on four scores, capsule included. As of this writing it holds 25 public entries; the median capsule score is 78/100 and the ten best average 84.8. The two highest capsule scores in the set are Hollow Knight: Silksong at 95 and Dota 2 at 90, with Hades, Stardew Valley, Hollow Knight, Red Dead Redemption 2, and Elden Ring clustered at 85.

Small sample, so treat it as a case list rather than a statistic. But the top scorers line up with the top-sellers tally: every one of them has a title readable at 120px, a single dominant subject or logotype, and one committed color decision (Silksong’s warm orange, Stardew’s blue, Hades’ red-on-black). None of them crams in a second scene, a tagline, or an award laurel - and Valve’s graphical asset rules ban taglines, review scores, and award logos on capsules anyway. The only text allowed is the game name and an official subtitle.

Patterns worth copying vs franchise privileges

The most useful way to read all of this is to split the patterns into two columns:

Copy this (indie-validated)Skip this (franchise privilege)

Getting this wrong is expensive. Your capsule is the click decision on every discovery surface Steam has - search, tag pages, the queue - and click-through moves the Steam algorithm’s opinion of you. The best documented single-variable case is still Imagine Earth, which replaced a weak capsule with a professional one during Early Access and went from 0-3 copies a day to 40-60 with no other change. Our CTR benchmarks post covers what a normal click-through rate looks like so you can tell whether your capsule is the bottleneck.

Run your own tally before you commission art

The whole method above is repeatable in an afternoon, and doing it for your genre beats trusting my global numbers, because capsule conventions are genre dialects. Before you brief an artist:

  1. 01Open your primary tag’s top-seller page and screenshot the first 50 small capsules.
  2. 02Tally the same five things I did: faces, characters, logotype-only, dark vs light, and text size.
  3. 03Note the two or three conventions nearly everyone follows. Those are genre signals; keep them (a hammer on a crafting game, per Zukowski’s trends piece).
  4. 04Note the visual space nobody occupies - if 40 of 50 are dark, a light capsule with the same genre props is your differentiation for free.
  5. 05Sketch text-first: title readable at 120x45, then build art around it. Check the slot dimensions in the capsule validator and the full design process in the capsule design guide.
  6. 06Test before you ship it: the capsule validator checks legibility at every render size, and if you are choosing between two finished candidates, our store page A/B testing guide covers how to measure the difference instead of guessing.

And if you want to see how your whole page stacks up against the games at the top, run it through the free analyzer and check your scores against the leaderboard. The capsule is one number in that report, but as 98 top sellers just demonstrated, it is the number shoppers see first.

Frequently asked questions

What do top-selling capsules have in common?

Across 98 top-seller capsules tallied on July 2, 2026, 97 of 98 put prominent, legible title text on the image. The highest capsule scores on the public leaderboard all share a title readable at 120px, one dominant subject or logotype, and a single committed color decision, whether the capsule leans on a face, a scene, or pure logotype.

Should the capsule show a character?

Only 20 of 98 top-seller capsules (20%) show a face large enough to read an expression, so “always show a character face” is a minority pattern, not a rule. A broader 40 of 98 (41%) include some figure, creature, or silhouette even without a readable face, and the honest version of the advice is conditional: a face helps when the genre sells on character, tone, or horror, and does nothing for a game like Euro Truck Simulator 2.

How much text belongs on a capsule?

Valve’s graphical asset rules only allow the game name and an official subtitle as text, banning taglines, review scores, and award logos. Among the top sellers, text spans more than half the capsule width on most of the 98, and for 24 of them the title text essentially is the entire composition, a logotype on a flat background with no scene or character.

Does the capsule need the game’s logo?

Almost always, yes. 97 of the 98 top-seller capsules put the title on the image, matching the Steamworks documentation’s own instruction that the logo should nearly fill the small capsule. The single exception, Subnautica 2, survives with no title text only because the franchise is already recognized.
